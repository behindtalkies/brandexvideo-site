How to Brief a Video Editor So the First Cut Is Right

✍️ Shankar, Creative Director📅 23 October 2026⏱ 7 min read

To brief a video editor well, answer seven questions on a single page: what the video should make the viewer do, who the viewer is, which platform it is for, one reference clip with a timestamp, what to keep and what to cut, the must-have elements, and the deadline. Seven answers, one page, about ten minutes. Editors are not on set with you, so the brief is the only context they have; the briefs that go wrong are not too short, they are vague, and "make it engaging" costs more revision rounds than any other sentence a client sends.

This guide is written for the business owner, creator or marketing lead who films on a phone and sends the footage to a remote editor. The same structure works whether that editor is a freelancer in your city or a studio ten time zones away, and whether you are briefing one clip or a batch of twenty.

What should a video editor brief include?

Every brief template we reviewed this month, from production-agency templates to an editor's own "platform basics" lessons, converges on the same core. The wording differs; the seven fields do not.

FieldWrite thisNot this
Goal"Get dentists in Texas to book a consult call""Brand awareness"
Audience"Clinic owners, 35–55, scrolling Instagram at night""Everyone"
Platform and format"Instagram Reels, 9:16, under 45 seconds""Social media"
ReferenceOne link plus "the caption style at 0:12"Five links with no notes
Keep and cut"Keep the story at 2:40; cut everything before 0:30""Use the good bits"
Must-havesLogo end card, website URL, brand blue #1A4FD8Nothing, then a revision to add them
Deadline"First cut by Thursday 9am EST""ASAP"

Put the goal first. An editor who knows the video exists to get consult bookings will end on the booking line and keep the pace tight around it. An editor who knows only the look will make something that looks right and does nothing.

Why "make it engaging" is the most expensive sentence in editing

Vague direction does not save you time; it moves the time to the revision rounds. The editor guesses, you react to the guess, and each round is a day lost. The production guides we read this month are blunt about this: specific feedback produces fewer rounds, and timestamped notes remove the most common cause of unnecessary revisions.

The fix is to replace every adjective with an outcome or a timestamp. "Punchier" becomes "cut the pauses longer than half a second and bring the b-roll in every 3 seconds." "More professional" becomes "match the colour grade of the reference at 0:08 and use the clean caption style, not the bouncing one." You are not expected to know editing vocabulary; you are expected to point at something.

The one-sentence test

If your brief does not contain a sentence of the form "this video should make [audience] [do one thing]", it is not finished. Everything else in the brief exists to serve that sentence.

How to give a reference without naming a creator

A reference is the most efficient part of a brief, but "make it like that famous guy's videos" fails twice: the editor has to guess which of his dozen styles you mean, and the style was built for his audience, not yours. Describe the mechanics instead.

  • Caption style: bold word-by-word captions that pop, or a clean single line that stays put? See our bold-caption style and clean educator style pages for what each looks like.
  • Pacing: a cut every 2–3 seconds with b-roll, or a slower cinematic talking-head style where the face carries it?
  • Colour: warm and filmic, or neutral and bright?
  • Hook: text on screen in the first second, or the spoken line?

One link with a timestamp and one line on what to take from it ("the caption placement at 0:12, not the music") does more than a paragraph of adjectives. Say what you dislike about the reference too; it narrows the target.

Keep-and-cut notes: the step most people skip

The editor was not in the room. They do not know that the second take was the one you liked, that the client's name is pronounced differently from the spelling, or that the tangent at 4:10 must never go public. Thirty seconds of notes per clip saves the editor an hour of hunting and saves you the awkward revision.

Three lines cover it: the timestamp of the best take, anything that must be cut for legal, compliance or tone reasons, and any phrase you want kept word for word. If a clip has a name, a drug, a price or a claim in it, flag it, because a confident caption with the wrong spelling is the error viewers remember.

How to brief a batch of 10 videos instead of one

Monthly plans run on batches, and a batch brief is different from a single-video brief. Instead of seven answers per clip, you write the seven once as a standing brief, then add one line per clip.

  1. Standing brief: goal, audience, platforms, caption style, colour, music feel, must-haves, deadline cadence. Written once, updated when something changes.
  2. Question list: the ten questions you answered on camera, numbered.
  3. File names that match: 2026-10-23_Q03_why-does-my-knee-click.mov. The editor can match every file to its question without opening it.
  4. Per-clip line: "Q03: best take is the second one; cut the cough at 0:45."

This is exactly how our remote editing workflow runs: a shared Google Drive folder with Raw, Review and Final sub-folders, the standing brief pinned at the top, and one line per clip. Clients in New York upload at night and have cuts in the Review folder by morning because the editor never has to ask what a file is.

If you are sending phone footage, two technical lines belong in the standing brief as well: film vertical in 4K, and leave two seconds of silence at the start and end of each clip. Our phone footage page covers the rest.

Feedback on the first cut: timestamps, not adjectives

The brief does not end when the first cut arrives. A good first cut is usually 80–90% there, and the last stretch is decided by how you give notes. Watch the cut once without pausing, then watch again and write one line per change with a timestamp.

"0:14, cut the pause before 'actually'. 0:32, caption says 'their', should be 'there'. 0:48, make the CTA bigger." Three lines, one round. "Can you make it flow better?" is three rounds. The editor reads your note, jumps to the second, makes the change, and moves on; nothing is interpreted.

Separate the two kinds of note. Corrections (spelling, a wrong clip, a missing logo) are the editor's job to fix without debate. Preferences (a different music track, a slower hook) are worth one sentence on why, so the editor can apply the same judgement to the next nine videos instead of waiting to be told again.

Frequently asked questions

What should a video editor brief include?

Seven things: the goal in one sentence, the audience, the platform and format, one reference link with a timestamp, keep-and-cut notes with timestamps, the must-have elements like logo and URL, and a specific deadline. It should fit on one page.

How long should a brief for a video editor be?

One page for a single video and about ten minutes to write. For a batch of videos, write one standing brief covering goal, audience, platform and style, then add one line per clip naming the best take and anything to cut.

How do I describe the style I want to a video editor?

Describe the mechanics rather than naming a creator: caption style, pacing, colour feel and hook type. Then send one reference link with a timestamp and one line on what to take from it and what to ignore.

What is the biggest mistake when briefing a video editor?

Vague direction such as "make it engaging" or "more professional". It forces the editor to guess, and each guess costs a revision round. Replace adjectives with outcomes and timestamps.

How should I give feedback on a first cut?

Watch it once without pausing, then write one line per change with a timestamp, for example "0:14, cut the pause". Separate corrections from preferences and give a one-sentence reason for preferences so the editor can apply it to the next videos.

Do I need to know editing terms to brief an editor?

No. You need to point at things: a timestamp in your footage, a timestamp in a reference, and a plain-language outcome such as "end on the booking link". The editor translates that into cuts, captions and grading.
