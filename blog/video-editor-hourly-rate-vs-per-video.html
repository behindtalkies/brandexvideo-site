Video Editor Hourly Rate vs Per-Video Pricing: Which Costs Less?

✍️ Shankar, Creative Director📅 14 October 2026⏱ 7 min read

In 2026 a freelance video editor's hourly rate runs $20–45 for a beginner, $45–85 for a mid-level editor and $85–150 for a senior one, with agencies billing $100–250 an hour. For short-form video, per-video pricing is almost always cheaper: a 60-second clip that takes two to four hours to edit costs $90–340 at mid-level hourly rates, against $75–200 as a flat per-video fee and $70–80 inside a monthly plan.

Hourly looks transparent. You see the rate, you see the hours, you pay for the work. But for Reels, TikToks and Shorts, where the deliverable is tightly defined and the work repeats every week, hourly billing quietly hands you the risk of a slow edit. This guide puts the 2026 rates side by side, works the sums on a real clip, and shows which pricing model wins in which situation.

How many hours does one short video actually take?

This is the variable that hourly quotes hide. The common rule of thumb is one to two hours of editing per finished minute, but short-form is denser than that. A 60-second Reel carries a reworked hook, hand-timed animated captions, colour correction, music and sound design, and often a thumbnail. In practice:

  • Basic cut (trim, auto-captions, music): 45 minutes to 1.5 hours.
  • Proper edit (hook rework, branded captions, grading, two revision rounds): 2–4 hours.
  • Specialist edit (motion graphics, B-roll sourcing, sound design): 4–6 hours and up.

Two editors at the same hourly rate can bill very different totals for the same clip. One has a caption template and a grading preset ready; the other builds both from scratch and charges you for the setup. Under hourly billing, the slower editor earns more. Under per-video billing, the faster one does.

When hourly pricing is the better deal

Hourly is not a trap. It fits a specific kind of work, and when your project matches, it is the fairer deal for both sides.

  • Undefined scope. You have 40 minutes of raw footage and no idea what the cuts should be. An editor working hourly can explore without padding a flat quote to cover the unknowns.
  • You are in the room. Live sessions, screen-shared edits and real-time direction are hourly work by nature.
  • One-off projects with heavy revisions. A brand film with four stakeholders signing off will blow through any revision cap. Hourly keeps that honest.
  • Repurposing a long asset. Cutting one webinar into an unknown number of clips is easier to price by the hour than by the clip until you know the yield.

One rate guide summarises it well: hourly pricing protects the editor, project pricing protects the buyer. The question is which protection you need.

When per-video or monthly pricing wins

For short-form video the deliverable is clear: one vertical clip, under 60 seconds, captioned, graded, ready to post. That is exactly the kind of work flat pricing is built for.

  • You post on a schedule. Two or more videos a week means the same brief, brand kit and footage style repeating. The editor gets faster every week; under hourly billing you never see that saving.
  • You need a budget you can defend. A fixed monthly fee is easier to approve than an invoice that varies by 300%.
  • Revisions are routine. Caption typos, a swapped clip, a different thumbnail. On hourly rates each one costs $15–40. Plans normally include unlimited revisions.
  • Turnaround matters. Hourly editors schedule you around other clients. Plans usually come with a fixed turnaround, 24 hours being standard in 2026.

The crossover is around five videos a month. Below that, a per-video freelancer is fine. Above it, a monthly plan is cheaper per video and removes the weekly hunt for availability. Our freelancer vs agency vs subscription breakdown covers the non-price trade-offs.

Questions to ask before you agree a rate

Ask these before the first invoice

For hourly: what is your estimate for one finished 60-second video, do you cap hours per clip, and are revisions billed at the same rate? For per-video: what counts as one video, how many revision rounds are included, and are captions, grading and thumbnails in the base price or extras? For either: what is the standard turnaround, and what does rush delivery cost?

Frequently asked questions

What is the average hourly rate for a video editor in 2026?

Freelance rates run $20–45 an hour for beginners, $45–85 for mid-level editors and $85–150 for senior editors. Published 2026 guides put the mid-level average near $58 and the senior average near $103. Agencies bill $100–250 an hour.

How many hours does it take to edit a 60-second video?

A basic trim with auto-captions takes under 1.5 hours. A proper short-form edit with a reworked hook, animated captions, colour correction and two revision rounds takes 2–4 hours. Motion graphics and sound design push it to 4–6 hours or more.

Is hourly or flat-rate video editing cheaper?

For short-form video, flat per-video pricing is usually cheaper and always more predictable. A 60-second Reel costs $90–340 at mid-level hourly rates but $75–200 as a flat fee, and $70–80 inside a monthly plan of 10 or more videos.

How much should I pay per video instead of per hour?

In 2026, $25–75 for a basic cut from a beginner, $75–200 for a proper edit from an experienced freelancer, and $200–500 from a specialist. Monthly plans bring that to $70–80 per video once you post five or more a month.

Do monthly video editing plans count as per-video pricing?

Effectively yes. A plan sets a fixed monthly fee for a set number of videos, so $799 for 10 videos is $79.90 each. Unlike per-video freelance quotes, plans usually include grading, captions, thumbnails and unlimited revisions in that figure.

Are overseas video editors cheaper per hour?

Listed hourly rates on the open freelance market are lower, often $15–30 an hour, but the hours billed per clip vary widely. Compare the all-in price per finished video, including revisions and turnaround, not the hourly rate.
