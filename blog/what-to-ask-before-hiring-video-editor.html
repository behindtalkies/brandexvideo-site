12 Questions to Ask a Video Editor Before You Hire Them

✍️ Shankar, Creative Director📅 26 October 2026⏱ 7 min read

The questions to ask a video editor before hiring fall into four groups: fit, process, terms and proof. Twelve questions, asked before any money changes hands, will tell you whether an editor can produce your kind of video, how fast, at what real cost, and what happens when something goes wrong. The last question, whether they will do a paid test edit on your own footage, settles more than the other eleven combined. Here is the full list, what a good answer sounds like, and the answers that should send you elsewhere.

This list works for a freelancer, a studio or a monthly subscription service, and for any platform. The wording of the good answers assumes short-form content for Reels, TikTok, Shorts and LinkedIn, because that is what most people hiring today need, but the logic holds for long-form too.

What should I ask about fit and style?

1. Can you show three edits in my format, for a business like mine? Not a showreel. A reel shows the most polished work across every style; you need to see vertical talking-head clips, or product demos, or whatever you actually publish. Three samples that look like your future videos beat thirty that do not.

2. Which caption, pacing and colour styles do you work in? A good editor describes mechanics: word-by-word bold captions, clean educator style with lower thirds, cinematic talking-head with a soft grade. If every sample has the same look and it is not yours, they have one style, not a range.

3. What do you do with footage shot on a phone? Most business owners film on a phone. The answer should mention exposure correction, stabilisation, audio clean-up and reframing to 9:16. "Phone footage is fine" with no detail usually means it gets captions and nothing else.

What should I ask about their process?

4. How do I send footage and how do you want it organised? Listen for a shared folder, a naming convention and a per-clip note. An editor who says "just WhatsApp it" has not thought about quality; see how remote video editing works for what a tidy process looks like.

5. What is your turnaround per video, and does it hold at volume? Ask for the number in hours, and then ask what happens when you send twenty clips at once. One editor can promise 24 hours for one clip and quietly take ten days for a batch. Also ask about rush work and what it costs, if it is offered at all.

6. How do we communicate, and how fast do you reply? You want a named channel, a stated response window and a time-zone answer. For overseas editors, a time difference is often an advantage: footage uploaded at night comes back by morning. It only works if someone replies when you are awake.

What should I ask about revisions, ownership and price?

7. How many revision rounds are included, and what counts as a revision? Hiring guides we reviewed this week agree on the red flag here: "we'll sort it out" is not a policy. A clear answer names a number or says unlimited, then defines the line between a revision (fix the caption at 0:32) and a new request (re-cut it for a different platform).

8. Who owns the raw footage, the project files and the finished videos? You should own all three. Ask whether you can get the project file if you leave, and where your footage is stored. An editor who hesitates is planning on lock-in.

9. What exactly is a "video" on your pricing, and what is included? Per-video pricing needs a length cap; monthly plans need a count. At BrandexVideo a video is up to 60 seconds and a longer one counts as two, and captions, colour grading, music, thumbnails and publishing are included. Whoever you ask, get the equivalent sentence in writing. Our pricing is published for exactly this reason: if a price needs a call to explain, ask why.

What should I ask about capacity and reliability?

10. What happens when you are ill, travelling or on holiday? A solo freelancer has one honest answer: your videos wait. A studio should name a second editor who already knows your brand. Neither answer is wrong, but you should know which one you are buying before a launch week.

11. How many clients are you editing for right now, and can you scale with me? The number itself matters less than whether they answer it. Follow up with "if I go from ten videos a month to forty, what changes?" The good answer involves more editor hours, not longer turnaround. Which single question matters most?

12. Will you do a short paid test edit on my footage and my brief? Everything above is a claim. A test edit is evidence. You send real clips, a one-page brief and a deadline, and you see what comes back: the style match, the turnaround, how they handle your revision notes, and how they communicate when it is your money on the line.

Insist on paying for it. An editor doing a free edit has every reason to over-deliver once and never again, and the ones with full books will decline, which filters your list in the wrong direction. Which answers are red flags?

Walk away when you hear

No samples in your format. "Unlimited" with no definition of a revision. A reluctance to put turnaround in hours. Vague ownership of project files. Delivery specs that only come up after you have paid. A refusal to do a paid test, or a push toward a long contract before one. Named-creator promises such as "I'll make you look like that famous guy": a professional describes the style, not the person.

None of these questions take more than a minute to ask, and a good editor will have heard every one of them before. The ones who answer quickly, specifically and in writing are the ones who run their edits the same way. Bring the list to the first call, then let the test edit make the decision.

Frequently asked questions

What should I ask a video editor before hiring them?

Ask for samples in your exact format, their turnaround per video and at volume, how many revisions are included and what counts as one, who owns the footage and project files, what a "video" means on their pricing, what happens when they are unavailable, and whether they will do a paid test edit on your footage.

Should I ask a video editor for a free trial?

No. Ask for a short paid test edit instead. A free edit tells you what an editor does once to win a client; a paid one on your real footage and brief shows the actual service, turnaround and communication you would be paying for.

How many revisions should a video editor include?

Enough that you are never charged for a caption typo or a trimmed pause. Many services offer unlimited revisions; the important part is a written definition of what counts as a revision versus a new request, such as re-cutting for a different platform.

Who owns the raw footage and project files after a video is edited?

You should. Confirm before hiring that you own the footage, the finished videos and the project files, and that you can take the project files with you if you stop working together.

What is a red flag when hiring a video editor?

Vague answers on revisions or turnaround, no samples in your format, reluctance to do a paid test edit, hesitation about who owns the project files, and a push toward a long contract before you have seen any work on your own footage.

How do I check a video editor's turnaround time is real?

Ask for it in hours, ask what happens with a batch of twenty clips, and then test it: a paid trial with a stated deadline shows whether the promised turnaround holds when it is your project.
