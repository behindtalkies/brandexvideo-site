Text Hook vs Spoken Hook: Which Stops the Scroll Faster?

✍️ Shankar, Creative Director📅 11 November 2026⏱ 6 min read

The text hook stops the scroll faster, because a reel is judged in the first second, often with the sound off. The spoken hook decides whether the viewer stays past second 3, which is the window Instagram's Skip Rate metric now measures. The reels that hold best use both: 3–8 words on frame one, and a spoken line that starts on frame one and says something the text did not.

Clients ask us this every week: should the hook be the words on screen or the words out of my mouth? They do different jobs at different moments, and treating them as rivals is why so many reels open with a line nobody reads and a line nobody hears. Here is what each hook does, when each wins, and how we layer them.

What is a text hook, and what is a spoken hook?

A text hook is the large on-screen line that appears on the very first frame of a reel, before anything else happens. It is not the caption track. Captions follow the speech word by word; the text hook is a single statement, set in your brand font, that sits in the upper-middle of the frame and tells the viewer what the next 30 seconds are about.

A spoken hook is the first sentence the viewer hears. In a well-cut reel it starts on frame one, with every breath, "hey guys" and intro trimmed off, so the first word lands at the same moment the text does.

Which one stops the scroll faster?

The text hook, and it is not close. Reels, TikToks and Shorts autoplay as the thumb moves, and a large share of that viewing happens with the sound off: on a commute, at a desk, in bed. In those moments a spoken hook does not exist. The viewer sees a face, a room and whatever text is on screen, and decides.

The decision is also faster than a spoken hook can work. A spoken line needs a second or two to reach its first meaningful word; a short text line is read as a single shape in a fraction of that. By the time your voice has said "so the thing about", the viewer has already read "Why your reels get 200 views" and chosen to stay.

This is why Instagram's own analytics have moved to measuring the first three seconds. In 2025 Instagram added a Skip Rate figure to Reels insights, defined as the percentage of viewers who scroll past within the first 3 seconds, alongside a retention chart that shows where people leave. If your skip rate is high, the problem is almost always the first frame, and the first frame is a text problem.

When does a spoken hook beat a text hook?

Once the viewer has stopped, the spoken hook takes over. Text can earn a second; it cannot hold eight. From roughly second 1 to second 3 the viewer is deciding whether the person talking is worth listening to, and that is a job for voice, face and the content of the sentence.

There are also cases where the spoken line is the stronger opener even on frame one:

  • When the visual is already the hook. A reveal, a transformation, a dramatic location or an unusual action stops the thumb on its own. Heavy text on top competes with it. A spoken line can run underneath without stealing the frame.
  • When your audience already follows you. Returning viewers recognise your face and voice and will give you the first second on trust. A fresh audience will not.
  • When the reel is sound-led. Formats built on a trending audio, a reaction or a spoken story are watched with sound on more often, which narrows the text hook's advantage.
  • When the line is personal. "I lost my biggest client last Tuesday" lands harder in your voice than in Helvetica. Keep the text to a short setup and let the speech carry the emotion.

Even then we rarely ship a reel with no text on frame one. A three-word label costs nothing and insures the muted viewer.

How do you layer the two without repeating yourself?

The most common mistake we see in raw footage and in self-edited reels is redundancy: the text says exactly what the mouth says. The muted viewer gets one message, the sound-on viewer gets the same message twice, and the first three seconds carry half the information they could.

The fix is to split the work. The text makes the promise; the speech raises the stakes or starts paying it off. Here is the pattern we cut to on every text hook reel and most talking-head edits.

MomentText on screenSpeechJob
Frame 13–8 word hook, upper-middleFirst word of the spoken hookStop the thumb, muted or not
0–3 sHook stays upSpoken line that adds to the textEarn the next five seconds
3–8 sHook drops, word-by-word captions beginThe problem or the first pointCarry the viewer over the 8–10 second cliff

A worked example. Text: "Your reels are too polite." Speech: "You spend ten seconds introducing yourself, and the viewer left at second two." The text is the claim; the speech is the evidence. Someone who reads only the text still knows what the video is about.

How many words, where, and in what style?

Short. We keep text hooks between three and eight words, and the shorter end performs better on a phone screen, where a 12-word line wraps into three rows and starts to look like a paragraph. If you cannot say it in eight words, you have two hooks; pick one.

Placement matters as much as wording. The hook sits in the upper-middle of the frame, clear of the username, caption and buttons that each platform lays over the bottom third and the right edge. A hook hidden behind the share button might as well not be there.

Use your brand font at a size that reads at arm's length, a colour that stands out from the background, and a subtle entrance (a pop, a typewriter or a quick slide) that finishes within half a second. Long animated reveals delay the message. Keep the hook visually distinct from the animated captions that follow, so the headline is never confused with the transcript.

How do you know which hook is working?

Read the retention chart and the skip rate together. A high skip rate with a steep drop in the first second points at the text hook and the first frame: wrong words, too many of them, or a frame that looks like every other reel. A reasonable skip rate followed by a slide between seconds 3 and 8 points at the spoken hook: the viewer stopped, listened, and was not convinced.

Test hooks, not videos

The cheapest experiment in short-form is the same footage with a different frame-one text. On every client's first batch we cut two or three hook variants of the same reel, publish the strongest, and keep the pattern that wins for the following month. Changing eight words costs minutes; refilming costs an afternoon.

Over a few weeks you will see which style your audience answers to: the blunt claim, the question, the specific number or the contradiction.

One last rule we hold on every talking-head edit: the hook, text or spoken, is never an introduction. No name, no logo, no "welcome back". The viewer did not stop for you yet. They stopped for the promise, and the first three seconds are for keeping it.

Frequently asked questions

What is a text hook on Reels?

A text hook is the short on-screen line, usually three to eight words, that appears on the first frame of a reel and states what the video is about. It is separate from the word-by-word captions and is designed to be read in under a second, including by viewers watching with the sound off.

Is a text hook or a spoken hook better?

The text hook stops the scroll faster because reels autoplay muted and a short line is read in a fraction of a second. The spoken hook is what keeps the viewer past second 3. The strongest reels use both, with the text making the promise and the speech adding to it rather than repeating it.

How long should a text hook be?

Three to eight words. Shorter lines read as a single shape on a phone screen, while longer lines wrap into several rows and slow the viewer down. If the idea needs more than eight words, split it so the text states the claim and the speech supplies the detail.

Should the text hook say the same thing as the spoken hook?

No. Repeating the spoken line on screen wastes the first three seconds. Let the text make the promise or the claim and let the speech raise the stakes, give the evidence or start the answer, so viewers with and without sound each get the full message.

What is Instagram's skip rate for Reels?

Skip rate is a metric Instagram added to Reels insights in 2025. It shows the percentage of viewers who scrolled past the reel within the first 3 seconds, and it sits alongside a retention chart that shows at which second viewers leave.

Can an editor add a text hook after filming?

Yes. The text hook is added in the edit, so it can be written, tested and changed without refilming. What filming needs to provide is a clean first frame with the speaker starting to talk immediately, so the editor can place the text and start the speech on frame one.
