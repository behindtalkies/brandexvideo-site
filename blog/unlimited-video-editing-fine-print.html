"Unlimited" Video Editing: What the Fine Print Actually Means

✍️ Shankar, Creative Director📅 4 November 2026⏱ 7 min read

"Unlimited" video editing almost never means unlimited videos. It means unlimited requests, worked one at a time, so the real ceiling is set by the turnaround per request: at 24–48 hours per clip, a single-queue plan delivers roughly 10–20 short videos a month, and considerably fewer if revisions are counted as new turns in the queue. The plan is worth buying only when you have read the five clauses that set that ceiling.

The word "unlimited" does real work on a pricing page. It removes the mental arithmetic of counting videos and lets a $1,000–$2,500 monthly fee feel like a bargain for anyone who publishes a lot. The problem is that output is never set by the word on the pricing page. It is set by five mechanics buried in the terms, and this guide walks through each one so you can calculate what a given plan will actually deliver before you sign.

What does "unlimited" actually refer to?

Read the disclaimer under the plan name and you will usually find that unlimited refers to requests, revisions and sometimes footage duration. It does not refer to completed videos. You may submit as many jobs as you like; the service decides how many it finishes.

The honest version of the sentence is: "You can queue as much work as you want, and we will work through it at our pace." That is a perfectly reasonable service. It just is not the same thing as a fixed number of edited videos, and the two are often priced as if they were.

One agency's own breakdown of how these plans operate is blunt about the result: a dedicated editor on an unlimited plan is typically shared across several clients, which works out to about an hour of editing time per client per day. Industry blogs that have run the numbers put the realistic output of a typical unlimited plan at three to four longer videos a month, or a somewhat higher count of short clips.

Clause 1: How many active requests at a time?

This is the clause that sets the ceiling, so find it first. Most unlimited plans allow one active request; higher tiers sometimes allow two. Everything else sits in a queue and does not start until the active job is marked complete.

The maths is simple. One active request at a 48-hour turnaround is at most 10 jobs in a 20-business-day month. One active request at 24 hours is at most 20. Two active requests at 24 hours is at most 40, which is where "unlimited" starts to resemble a real volume plan, and that tier is rarely the headline price.

Compare that against a plan that simply states its number. BrandexVideo's plans are 10, 20 or 40 videos a month at $799, $1,499 and $2,799, each clip up to 60 seconds, with a 24-hour standard turnaround. The count is the count, and you can check it against the invoice.

Clause 2: Is turnaround a promise or an estimate?

Pricing pages say "24–48 hour turnaround." Terms pages often add that turnaround times are estimates, vary with complexity, and exclude weekends and public holidays. All three qualifiers are reasonable on their own; together they can stretch a two-day promise into a five-day reality during a busy week.

Ask two questions before you buy. Is the quoted time to first draft or to final file? Is it measured in calendar hours or business days? A 48-hour business-day turnaround that lands on a Friday afternoon is a Tuesday delivery, which matters a great deal if you post daily.

Clause 3: What counts as a revision?

"Unlimited revisions" is true at nearly every service, including ours. The fine print is about when revisions happen, not whether they do. On a single-queue plan a revision round usually re-enters the queue as the active request, so a clip that needs two rounds of notes consumes three turnaround cycles, and nothing else moves while it does.

Some terms also distinguish a revision from a "new request." Changing the music is a revision; changing the hook, the aspect ratio or the footage used is often classed as a new edit and queued accordingly. Neither rule is wrong, but you should know which one you are buying into, because it decides whether "three rounds of feedback" costs you three days or a week.

Clauses 4 and 5: scope caps, pausing and cancellation

Footage length, video length and format caps

Even plans that advertise unlimited footage duration usually have a soft rule about scope. Common ones include a cap on raw footage per request (an hour of source material is typical), a cap on finished video length (anything over a set number of minutes counts as two requests), and a limit on the number of output formats per request (one aspect ratio included, extra crops queued separately).

Transparent plans state these rules plainly. On BrandexVideo's plans, for example, every video is up to 60 seconds and a longer video counts as two. That rule is restrictive on purpose, because it keeps the per-video price honest, and it is written on the pricing page rather than in the terms.

The five-question fine-print check

Before paying for any unlimited plan, get written answers to: (1) How many requests are active at once? (2) Is turnaround to draft or to final, in hours or business days? (3) Does a revision re-enter the queue? (4) What raw-footage and output-length caps apply? (5) Can I pause, and what is the notice period for cancelling? If any answer is "it depends," model the worst case.

Billing, pausing and cancellation

Unlimited plans are usually billed monthly in advance, and the headline price is often the annual-contract rate with the month-to-month price 15–25% higher. Check whether the plan can be paused in a light month, whether unused days roll over (they almost never do), and how much notice cancellation requires.

The quiet cost of a single-queue plan is the month you only need four videos. You pay the full fee because the subscription does not know you are busy with something else, and the queue sat idle. A per-video plan with a known count makes that cost visible; an unlimited plan hides it.

Unlimited plans suit two kinds of buyer well: a creator who wants exactly one video in progress at a time and approves drafts the same day, and a business that values a dedicated editor's brand familiarity more than raw volume. If you post daily, batch-film, or need several clips moving in parallel, a plan that states its number is easier to budget and easier to hold to account. Frequently asked questions

Is unlimited video editing really unlimited?

No. Unlimited refers to the number of requests you can submit and the number of revisions you can ask for, not to the number of finished videos. Output is capped by how many requests are active at once and the turnaround on each, so a single-queue plan at 48 hours per request delivers around ten jobs a month at most.

How many videos do you get from an unlimited video editing plan?

It depends on the active-request limit and turnaround. One active request at a 24-hour turnaround gives a theoretical maximum of about 20 short clips in a 20-business-day month, and fewer once revisions take their turn in the queue. Plans with a 48-hour turnaround usually land closer to 6–10.

What does 'one active request at a time' mean?

Only one job is being edited at any moment. Every other request you submit waits in a queue and does not start until you approve or close the current one. It is the single most important clause in an unlimited plan because it, not the price, determines your monthly output.

Do revisions count against an unlimited video editing plan?

Revisions are usually unlimited in number but on most plans each revision round re-enters the queue as the active request, so a clip that needs two rounds of changes uses three turnaround cycles. Check whether larger changes such as a new hook or a different aspect ratio are classed as a new request.

Is a fixed-count video editing plan better than an unlimited one?

For anyone publishing more than a handful of videos a month, a plan that states its count is easier to budget and verify. BrandexVideo's plans are 10, 20 or 40 videos a month at $799, $1,499 and $2,799 with a 24-hour standard turnaround and unlimited revisions, so the number of videos is on the invoice rather than implied by a queue.

What should I ask before buying an unlimited video editing subscription?

Ask how many requests are active at once, whether turnaround is to draft or final and in hours or business days, whether revisions re-enter the queue, what raw-footage and output-length caps apply, and whether you can pause the plan. Get the answers in writing and model the slowest case before paying.
