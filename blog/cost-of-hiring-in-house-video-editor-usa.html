What an In-House Video Editor Really Costs a US Business in 2026

✍️ Shankar, Creative Director📅 16 October 2026⏱ 7 min read

Hiring a full-time video editor in the USA costs a business roughly $90,000–$120,000 in the first year. The salary alone averages $66,000–$79,000 in 2026 depending on the source, and benefits and employer taxes add about 30% on top before you count recruiting, software and hardware. Spread across the 20–40 short videos a month most businesses actually publish, that works out to $190–$400 per video, against $70–$80 per video on a monthly editing plan.

Most owners budget for the salary and stop there. The salary is the biggest line, but it is not the bill. This guide adds up every cost of an in-house editor in the USA, divides it by the number of videos you will realistically get, and shows where the break-even sits against an outsourced plan.

What is the average video editor salary in the USA in 2026?

The published averages disagree by about $13,000, which tells you how much location and experience matter. ZipRecruiter puts the national average at $65,728 a year (about $31.60 an hour) as of October 2026, with the middle half of editors earning roughly $44,500–$82,500. Indeed's job-posting data gives an average of $33.72 an hour, which is about $70,000 for a full-time year. Glassdoor's figure, as cited in 2026 industry pricing guides, is around $79,000, with a typical range of $60,000–$103,000.

Use the low end if you are hiring in a lower-cost state and are happy with two to three years of experience. Use the high end for New York, Los Angeles, Austin or San Francisco, or for an editor who also does motion graphics and sound design.

What does an employee cost beyond salary?

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics measures this every quarter. In March 2026, private-industry employers paid $46.60 an hour in total compensation, of which $32.60 was wages and $14.01 was benefits. Benefits were 30.1% of total compensation, which is a 43% add-on to the wage itself.

A video editor's benefits are usually lighter than the all-industry average, so a 25–35% add-on is a sensible planning figure. It covers the employer's share of Social Security (6.2%) and Medicare (1.45%), federal and state unemployment insurance, health insurance, paid leave and any retirement match. The Small Business Administration's rule of thumb, repeated in most 2026 hiring guides, is that a fully loaded employee costs 1.25 to 1.5 times base salary.

What are the one-off costs of hiring a video editor?

These are the ones nobody budgets for.

  • Recruiting. SHRM's widely cited benchmark for the average cost per hire is about $4,700, covering job ads, screening time and interviews. Use a recruiter and the fee is typically a percentage of first-year salary.
  • Hardware. A workstation that handles 4K short-form footage without stalling, plus fast storage and a calibrated monitor. Budget a few thousand dollars, more if they shoot too.
  • Software and assets. An editing suite subscription, a caption or transcription tool, licensed music and stock footage, plus cloud storage for raw files. These are monthly and they add up over a year.
  • Ramp-up. An editor's first month is spent learning your brand, your hook style and your approval chain. You pay full salary for a month of partial output.
  • Management time. Someone has to brief, review, give feedback and run performance reviews. For a founder, that is the most expensive hour in the company.

How much does that work out to per video?

This is the number that matters, and it depends entirely on volume. A full-time editor has about 160 working hours a month. A properly finished short-form video, with a reworked hook, animated captions, colour correction and a revision round, takes two to four hours, as we set out in our guide to hourly versus per-video pricing. After meetings, admin, asset hunting and re-exports, 40 finished shorts a month is a strong month for one person, and 20 is common.

When does hiring in-house make sense?

It does, in three situations. First, when you need more than about 60 finished videos a month across several brands and the editor stays saturated. Second, when the role is really an in-house producer who also shoots, directs talent and runs the content calendar, so the salary is buying far more than editing. Third, when your footage is sensitive enough that it cannot leave the building, which is rare for marketing content.

The honest break-even

At $95,000 a year all-in, an in-house editor only beats a $2,799-a-month plan on price once they deliver more than about 113 finished videos a month, which no single editor does. Below that, you are paying for availability, not output. That can be worth it, but it is a choice, not a saving.
