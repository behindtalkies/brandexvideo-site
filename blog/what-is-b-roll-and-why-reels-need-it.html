What Is B-Roll and Why Every Reel Needs It

✍️ Shankar, Creative Director📅 6 November 2026⏱ 6 min read

B-roll is any supplementary footage shown over your main shot while the original audio keeps playing: hands typing, the clinic doorway, the product on the desk, a screen recording. You talking to camera is the A-roll; B-roll is everything the editor cuts to while your voice carries on. Reels need it because one unchanging frame gives the eye nothing new to land on, which is why in our edits something on screen changes every 3–5 seconds.

If an editor has ever asked you to "send some b-roll" and you were not sure what to film, this guide is for you. It covers what b-roll is, where the odd name comes from, why short-form video depends on it, what to shoot for your type of business, and how much a 60-second reel really needs.

What is b-roll, exactly?

B-roll is secondary footage that supports the main footage. In a talking-head reel the main footage, or A-roll, is you speaking to the lens. The b-roll is the set of extra shots laid over the top while your voice continues underneath.

It is usually shown without its own sound, or with a little ambient sound mixed low. It does not have to be filmed on the same day or even by you. Screen recordings, photos, clips from earlier videos and stock footage all count when they are used this way.

Where does the name come from?

The term is a leftover from film editing. On 16 mm film, splices showed up as flaws in the picture, so editors split alternate shots across two rolls, an A-roll and a B-roll, and printed from both to hide the joins. In the 1980s the name carried over to video editing suites with two tape decks, where the "B" deck held the additional footage that was cut in without sound.

The equipment is long gone. The idea is the same: one source carries the story, the other gives the editor something to cut to.

Why does every reel need b-roll?

B-roll does four jobs in a short video, and a good insert usually does more than one of them at once.

  • It resets attention. A talking head on its own is a static frame. A new image gives the viewer a reason to keep looking.
  • It hides cuts. When an editor removes a stumble or tightens a sentence, the join is a visible jump. An insert laid over the join makes it disappear.
  • It shows proof. If you say the kitchen was rebuilt in a week, the viewer wants to see the kitchen. Footage of the thing is more convincing than a description of it.
  • It adds context. A shot of the clinic, the workshop or the office tells people who you are and where you work in under a second.

"Every reel" has honest exceptions. When your facial reaction is the point, or the style is deliberately unedited, cutting away weakens the moment. Even then, one or two inserts are usually what make a tight edit possible.

What counts as b-roll for your kind of business?

You do not need cinematic footage. You need shots that match what you talk about. These are the kinds of inserts that work.

Who you areB-roll worth filming
Doctor, dentist or clinicClinic entrance, hands preparing equipment, an anatomical model, writing notes
Coach or consultantNotebook and pen, whiteboard, laptop screen recording, walking into a session
Real estate agentFront door opening, room walkthrough, street view, keys changing hands
Restaurant or food businessPlating, a pour, flames on the pass, the dining room filling up
SaaS founderProduct screen recording, dashboard close-up, the team at work
Anyone on cameraHands, workspace, tools of the trade, the same scene from a side angle

The side angle in the last row is the most underused shot. Filming yourself from a second position while you work gives an editor a cutaway that matches any sentence you say.

How much b-roll does a 60-second reel need?

There is no fixed ratio of A-roll to b-roll and no required number of cuts per minute. Adobe's own guide to b-roll makes the same point: a short social reel may need only a few seconds of cutaways, while a documentary needs a great deal.

Our working rule is that something on screen should change every 3–5 seconds. That change can be a b-roll insert, a punch-in on the speaker or a caption beat, so b-roll does not have to carry the whole load. What matters more than quantity is placement: an insert that lands on the key word feels intentional, and one that lands mid-sentence looks random.

The amount also depends on the look you want. A cinematic talking-head style uses fewer, slower inserts; a fast-cut entertainment style barely holds a frame. You can compare all six approaches on our editing styles page.

Cutting to new footage every couple of seconds just to follow a rule makes a video feel busy. If the A-roll is already making the point, leave it alone.

How do you film b-roll on a phone?

B-roll for a business reel can be shot on the same phone as the main video, in ten spare minutes after filming. A short list is enough.

A 7-shot b-roll list for any talking-head video

(1) Wide shot of your workspace. (2) Close-up of your hands doing the work. (3) The main tool or product, filmed slowly. (4) You walking in or sitting down. (5) Your screen, or a screen recording. (6) One detail that shows the place: a sign, a shelf, a view. (7) You working, filmed from the side.

Five habits make that footage usable:

  • Hold every shot for ten seconds. A director quoted in Adobe's guide advises counting to ten in your head, so the editor has room to trim.
  • Film vertically if the reel is vertical, and move the phone slowly or not at all.
  • Shoot in the same light as your main video so the two can be graded to match.
  • Clean the lens and use the rear camera at the highest quality setting.
  • Send everything. Editors would rather choose from twenty clips than stretch three.

For the main shot itself, see our guide to filming talking-head videos at home. If phone footage is all you have, that is fine: it is exactly what our phone video editing is for.

Should you use stock footage instead?

Use your own footage wherever you can. Real shots of your clinic, your product or your hands build trust in a way that generic stock cannot, and viewers recognise overused stock clips quickly. Stock is useful for things you cannot film, such as a city skyline or an abstract concept, and should be the exception.

Frequently asked questions

What does b-roll mean?

B-roll is supplementary footage that is shown over the main shot while the main audio continues. In a talking-head video, the speaker on camera is the A-roll and the cutaway shots of hands, products, places or screens are the b-roll.

What is the difference between A-roll and b-roll?

A-roll is the primary footage that carries the story, usually a person speaking to camera or an interview. B-roll is the secondary footage laid over it to illustrate what is being said, hide cuts and add visual variety.

Why is it called b-roll?

The name comes from 16 mm film editing, where alternate shots were split across two rolls, an A-roll and a B-roll, to hide splices when printing. The term carried over to video editing in the 1980s, when a second tape deck labelled B held the additional footage.

Do talking-head reels need b-roll?

Almost always. B-roll resets the viewer's attention, covers the cuts where mistakes were removed and shows proof of what the speaker is describing. The exceptions are moments where the speaker's reaction is the point or the style is deliberately raw.

How long should a b-roll clip be?

When filming, hold each shot for about ten seconds so the editor has room to trim. In a finished reel an insert normally stays on screen only long enough to cover one phrase before the edit returns to the speaker or moves to the next image.

Can I use stock footage as b-roll?

Yes, but your own footage is better for building trust. Stock footage works for things you cannot film yourself, such as a skyline or an abstract idea, and should be used sparingly because viewers recognise generic clips.
