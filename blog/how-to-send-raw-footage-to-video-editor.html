How to Send Raw Footage to a Video Editor (Without Losing Quality)

✍️ Shankar, Creative Director📅 26 October 2026⏱ 7 min read

The way to send raw footage to a video editor without losing quality is to upload the original files, untrimmed and unfiltered, to a shared cloud folder such as Google Drive or Dropbox, then send the editor the link. Never send footage through WhatsApp, iMessage or email: those tools compress video to fit their size caps, and the editor receives a soft, blocky copy that no amount of grading can repair. The rest of this guide covers which methods damage your clips, how big the files really are, and how to name them so the editor knows what each one is before opening it.

This is written for the business owner or creator who films on a phone and sends the clips to a remote editor, whether a freelancer or a studio on the other side of the world. The method is the same for one clip and for a batch of thirty.

Why does my footage lose quality when I send it?

Because most of the tools people reach for first were built for sharing, not delivering. Messaging apps and email re-encode video to fit a size limit, and the loss is permanent. Pages we checked this week put WhatsApp's video cap at roughly 16 MB per clip and typical email attachment limits at around 25 MB, which is less than a single minute of 1080p phone footage.

The result is a file that looks fine on a phone screen and falls apart the moment an editor punches in, slows down a moment, or pushes the colour. Captions still work. Everything that makes a clip look produced does not.

iMessage is a quieter problem. Between two Apple devices it can send full quality, but to anyone on Android it downgrades, and many editors do not use iPhones. Treat it as unreliable rather than wrong.

What is the best way to send raw footage to a video editor?

A shared cloud folder the editor can access directly. You upload the originals, the editor downloads the originals, and nothing in between touches the file. Google Drive and Dropbox both do this; transfer services such as WeTransfer also preserve quality but their links expire after days or weeks, which is awkward when you want to re-use a clip later.

One detail that catches people out on iPhone: when you share from the Photos app, some share sheets offer a size choice. Always pick the original or "actual size" option. Uploading through the Drive or Dropbox app directly avoids the question entirely.

How big are raw video files, and how long will the upload take?

Bigger than most people expect, and that is a good sign. Published estimates for recent iPhones put a minute of 1080p at 30 fps at about 60 MB in HEVC, a minute of 4K at 30 fps at roughly 170 MB, and 4K at 60 fps at around 400 MB. A batch of thirty one-minute clips shot in 4K at 30 fps is therefore about 5 GB.

Upload time depends entirely on your connection's upload speed, which is usually far slower than its download speed. The practical answer is to upload on Wi-Fi, start it before you walk away, and let it run overnight. For clients in the US or UK working with an editor in Asia, this lines up neatly: footage uploaded at bedtime is downloaded at the start of the editor's day.

Do not reduce resolution to make the upload faster. A 4K source lets the editor crop, reframe and punch in without the picture going soft. A 1080p source still edits well, but every punch-in costs sharpness. If you have to choose, keep 4K at 30 fps; 60 fps is only worth the file size for slow-motion shots.

Should I trim, filter or compress clips before sending?

No, to all three. Trimming on the phone throws away the half-second of handle an editor needs for a clean cut, and it is easy to clip the first word. Filters bake a look into the file that cannot be undone, and they fight the colour grade. Compression apps exist to make files small enough for messaging, which is exactly the problem you are avoiding.

The same goes for the phone's own "Most Compatible" versus "High Efficiency" setting. Either is fine; professional editing software opens both. Leave the setting where it is and send what the camera produced.

Two things are worth doing before you send. Delete the obvious failures, such as the take where the dog barked, and leave the rest. And if a clip is longer than a few minutes, leave it whole rather than cutting it into pieces; the editor would rather scrub one file than reassemble six.

How should I name and organise the files?

Phones name clips IMG_4821.MOV, which tells the editor nothing. Renaming takes a minute per batch and saves the editor from opening every file to find out what is in it. The pattern we ask for is date, then a clip number, then a few words on the topic:

  • 2026-10-26_02_three-things-to-check.mov
  • 2026-10-26_03_pilot-explainer.mov

The date keeps the list in shooting order, the number matches the clip to your question list or brief, and the topic lets the editor find "the pricing one" without opening anything. If you shoot on two devices, add a short tag such as A or B so the editor can match angles.

Inside the folder, keep it flat: one Raw folder per shoot date is plenty. Drop a short text file or a Google Doc in the same folder with one line per clip, the platform it is for, and any must-haves. That doc is your brief; how to brief a video editor covers what goes in it.

What should I send alongside the footage?

Upload checklist

Original clips, untrimmed, no filters. A one-line note per clip. Your logo as a PNG with a transparent background. Brand colours as hex codes and the font name. One reference link with a timestamp. The platform and deadline for the batch. Any music preference, or "editor's choice".

Send the brand assets once; a good editor keeps them on file and you never send them again. Send the per-clip notes every time. The combination of clean originals and a clear note is what lets a first cut come back right, and it matters more than the camera you used. Most of what we receive is phone footage, and the clips that look produced on the way out are the ones that arrived uncompressed with a sentence of context.

Frequently asked questions

Can I send raw footage to a video editor through WhatsApp?

No. WhatsApp compresses video heavily to fit a small per-clip cap, and the loss is permanent. Use WhatsApp for notes and timestamps, and upload the footage itself to a shared Google Drive or Dropbox folder.

What is the best way to send large video files to an editor?

A shared cloud folder such as Google Drive or Dropbox, which stores and delivers the original file unchanged. Transfer services like WeTransfer also keep full quality but their links expire, so a permanent folder is better for ongoing work.

Does AirDrop reduce video quality?

No, AirDrop sends the original file. It only helps if the editor is physically nearby, which rules it out for a remote editor; use a cloud folder instead.

Should I film in 4K before sending footage to an editor?

Yes if your phone supports it. 4K gives the editor room to crop, reframe and punch in without the image going soft. 4K at 30 fps is the sweet spot; 60 fps roughly doubles the file size and is only worth it for slow motion.

How should I name video files for my editor?

Use the shoot date, a clip number and a few words on the topic, for example 2026-10-26_03_pricing-explainer.mov. The date keeps clips in order, the number matches your brief, and the topic lets the editor find a clip without opening it.

Should I trim my clips before sending them to the editor?

No. Send the full take. Trimming on the phone removes the handles an editor needs for clean cuts and often clips the first word. Just delete the obvious failed takes and leave the rest untouched.
