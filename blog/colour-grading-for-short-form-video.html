Colour Grading for Short-Form Video: Why Your Reels Look Flat

✍️ Shankar, Creative Director📅 13 November 2026⏱ 6 min read

Reels look flat for two reasons: the footage was graded before it was corrected, or it was never corrected at all. Colour grading for short-form video is a two-step process: correct white balance, exposure and contrast first, then add a look that survives a phone screen and platform compression. Most self-edited reels skip step one, stack a filter on top, and end up grey, orange or washed out after upload.

Colour is the fastest tell between a self-edited reel and one that has been through an editor. The content can be identical; the graded version simply looks finished. This post explains why phone footage comes out flat, why it looks different again once Instagram or TikTok has processed it, and the order of operations we use on every short video.

Why does phone footage look flat before grading?

Modern phones make choices for you, and most of them trade punch for safety. iPhones have recorded HDR video by default since the iPhone 12, which captures a wider brightness range than a standard screen can show. When that file lands in an editor that is working in a standard (SDR) timeline, the highlights are compressed and the midtones lift, and the image reads as milky.

Auto exposure and auto white balance add a second problem. Walk past a window mid-sentence and the phone re-exposes; a warm lamp and a cool screen in the same room pull skin two different directions. Each clip is individually acceptable, but when you cut them together the face changes colour on every jump cut.

Finally, phones apply their own gentle processing in camera, so there is less contrast left to work with than a mirrorless file would have. None of this is a reason to buy a camera. It is a reason to correct before you grade, which is what the colour grading step in our edits is for.

Why do reels look washed out or too bright after uploading?

This is the complaint we hear most from clients who film on an iPhone: the clip looked great in the camera roll and arrived on Instagram brighter and flatter. Two things happen between export and playback.

First, HDR handling. Meta's engineering team has described how Reels generates both an HDR and an SDR version of every HDR upload on its servers, and devices without HDR support receive the tone-mapped SDR copy. Tone mapping is an approximation of the creator's intent, and Meta itself notes that poor tone mapping can push a video too bright or shift colours. If you graded in HDR and your audience watches in SDR, you are not seeing the same video they are.

Second, compression. Platforms re-encode every upload at a far lower bitrate than the original file, and heavy compression crushes shadow detail and bands smooth gradients. A grade with very deep blacks and subtle skies has the most to lose.

The practical fix is to take control of the pipeline rather than hope the platform guesses well. Either switch HDR off before filming (Settings, Camera, Record Video, HDR Video off on iPhone), or have your editor convert the HDR footage to a standard colour space deliberately and grade it there. Then export a short test clip and watch it on a phone before posting the real thing.

Colour correction vs colour grading: what is the difference?

People use the two words interchangeably, but they are different jobs done in a fixed order. Correction makes the footage accurate. Grading makes it intentional. Skipping correction and jumping to a look is the single most common reason a reel looks amateur.

Notice that grading is the smallest of the three steps. Most of the visible improvement in a reel comes from correction and the platform check, not from a dramatic look.

How do editors grade a short-form video in practice?

Our editors follow the same sequence on every clip, because order is what keeps 40 videos a month consistent. White balance comes first: neutralise the greys so the face is not green under office lighting or orange under a lamp. Exposure second: bring the face to a natural brightness and let the background fall where it falls.

Contrast third, and gently. Short-form video is watched on a six-inch screen in daylight, so a lifted midtone with moderate blacks reads better than a cinematic crushed-black look that turns to mud after compression. Skin tones are checked next, by isolating the skin hue range and pulling saturation back if the grade has pushed it towards sunburn.

Only then does the look go on, and the look is one preset per client, refined over time, applied to every video. If you have been wondering how editors make iPhone footage look professional, this consistency is a bigger part of it than any individual setting.

The phone-size check

Never approve a grade on a desktop monitor alone. Export, send it to your phone, and watch it at arm's length with the brightness you normally use. If the face still looks like a face and the captions are readable, it will survive the platform.

Common colour grading mistakes that make reels look amateur

Stacking looks is the first. A graded export with an in-app Instagram filter added on top is two grades fighting, and the result is almost always oversaturated skin. Post the graded file and leave the app's filters alone.

Grading clip by clip is the second. Each shot is tuned in isolation, so individually they are fine and together they flicker. Editors set one reference frame and match every other clip to it before touching the look.

Chasing a new LUT every week is the third. A LUT is a starting point built for someone else's camera, and rotating them makes a profile feel like five different people filmed it. Pick one look and keep it. Oversaturation and heavy contrast round out the list: both look dramatic on a large monitor and fall apart on a small screen after compression.

What to send your editor so grading is easy

Good colour starts on set, and the set is usually a desk. Film with one dominant light source rather than a lamp plus a window plus a screen. Lock exposure and focus before you start talking (press and hold on the subject in the iPhone camera app) so the phone stops re-deciding mid-take.

Decide on HDR once and stick to it for the whole batch; a mix of HDR and SDR clips is the hardest footage to match. Send the original files rather than a compressed messaging-app copy, because the editor needs the full range to correct from. Our guide to getting phone videos edited covers the transfer side.

Finally, mention the look you want in one sentence, with a reference from your own feed if you have one: warm and natural, clean and bright, or moody and cinematic. The editor will build the preset once and every video after that will match.

Frequently asked questions

Why do my reels look flat and washed out?

Usually because the footage was recorded in HDR with auto exposure, then uploaded without correction. The platform tone-maps HDR to SDR and compresses the file, which lifts midtones and crushes detail. Correcting white balance, exposure and contrast before upload, and testing a clip on a phone, fixes most of it.

Should I turn off HDR video on my iPhone for reels?

If you edit your own videos and your editor is not managing colour space deliberately, yes: switching HDR Video off in the iPhone camera settings gives you footage that looks the same in the camera roll, the timeline and the app. If a professional editor is handling the footage, keep whichever setting you choose consistent across the whole batch.

What is the difference between colour correction and colour grading?

Colour correction makes footage accurate and consistent: neutral white balance, correct exposure, matched clips. Colour grading adds a deliberate look on top, such as warmth, a shadow tint or a particular contrast curve. Correction always comes first.

Why does Instagram make my video brighter?

Instagram does not add a brightness filter; the change comes from converting HDR footage to SDR for most screens and from re-encoding the file at a lower bitrate. Both lift midtones and flatten contrast. Posting an SDR, moderately contrasted export with a slightly lower brightness than looks right on a desktop monitor usually lands correctly.

Do I need a LUT to colour grade short-form video?

No. A LUT is a preset built for someone else's camera and lighting, and most reels look better with simple correction plus a light, consistent look. If you do use one, use the same one on every video so the account looks like one person filmed it.
